Curtain Cord-Loop Drive

A geared motor at the stack end of the existing rod turns a closed cord loop, and one clamp ties the curtain's lead ring to the top run of that loop. Every other ring stays free and follows the fabric. The design only works if nothing else touches the cord. The loop has to run behind and below the rings, hooks and rod brackets with room to spare, because a cord that rubs one ring drags the whole curtain.

Proposal, nothing boughtPart sizes on this page are suggestions: a 12V geared DC motor of the JGA25-370 class at about 60–100 RPM, a 20 mm drive pulley, a non-stretch braided cord and two lever microswitches. None of them has been measured against the real rod yet. The PROJECTS.md entry still recommends a clip-on rod robot and calls DIY "not the plan". This page draws the DIY option so the two can be compared.

The whole drive, from the front

How it mounts

Two views of the new hardware at each end of the rod. On the left, the motor end, looking along the rod from beyond its left end. Here the drawing plots distance from the wall across and height up. On the right, the idler end, seen from the front at a larger scale than the whole-drive drawing.

Measure the bracket before buying pulleysThe cord has to pass every rod bracket. A bracket arm can come off the wall at rod height with a base plate that reaches down, or a centre support can hang below the rod. Either can sit in the cord's path. The pulleys go outside the end brackets. A centre bracket on a long rod blocks the cord completely, and it also stops rings, so check the rod has none. If the arm only leaves 20–30 mm below the rod, lower both pulleys rather than moving the cord forward into the rings.

The lead ring clamp

Only one ring touches the cord. The same end view for the lead ring and for any other ring shows the difference: the lead ring has an arm and a cord lock, and the others have nothing near the cord.

How it moves

The two runs of a loop always travel in opposite directions. Whichever run the lead ring is clamped to carries it. The other run just returns. The hollow diamond marks one point on the bottom run, so you can watch it move the other way.

End-of-travel switches

Two lever microswitches tell the controller the curtain is fully open or fully closed. Something on the moving side has to press each lever at the right moment. That can be the lead ring's own arm, or a bead crimped on the cord. Both layouts use the same parts. They differ in where the switches sit and how far their cables run.

Chosen: B (2026-09-25). Both switches and the controller sit together at the motor end, so no switch cable runs along the rod or hides behind the stacked fabric. The beads can be moved along the cord to fine-tune where each end stops.

A missed switch is a stallIf a switch does not trip, the motor keeps pulling until something gives. That could be the cord lock slipping, the lead ring tearing out of the fabric or the idler plate pulling out of the wall. Give the controller a maximum run time a few seconds longer than one full travel (see the numbers below), so a missed switch costs a pause, not a ring. Set each switch so it clicks before the lead ring reaches the bracket or squeezes the stack, never at the moment of contact.

The numbers, as proposals

Travel speed is the drive pulley's circumference times its RPM. With a 20 mm pulley, one turn moves the cord 63 mm. The force figure is an estimate for sizing the motor, not a measurement: a 3 kg curtain on rings with friction around 0.3 needs about 9 N of pull. At the pulley's 10 mm radius that is 0.09 N·m, about 0.9 kg·cm. Choose a motor rated for several times that, and weigh the real curtain first.

pulley Ø20 mm motor 60–100 RPM cord speed 63–105 mm/s 1.5 m travel 14–24 s pull, 3 kg curtain ~9 N torque at pulley ~0.9 kg·cm

If the cord slips and a GT2 belt loop replaces it, a 20-tooth GT2 pulley has a 12.7 mm pitch diameter, so each turn moves 40 mm. At 60–100 RPM that is 40–67 mm/s, or 22–37 s for 1.5 m. The belt is slower, and it cannot slip.

Slip at the drive pulley looks like a motor problemA cord on a smooth pulley grips only through tension and wrap. Under a heavy curtain it slips: the motor turns, the pulley turns and the curtain stops halfway. Use a deep V-groove or a knurled pulley, and a spring strong enough to keep the idler off the end of its slot. If it still slips, give the cord a full extra turn around the drive pulley, or move to a GT2 belt. Check for slip first: mark the cord and the pulley with a pen and watch whether the marks separate.

A geared motor will not let the curtain be pulled by handA JGA25 gearbox resists being turned backward. Tugging the fabric loads the cord lock, the arm and the lead ring's stitching instead. Plan for that from the start. Either the cord lock is set to slip before the fabric tears, or the curtain is only ever moved through the controller.

Check in this order

Build and check the mechanics by hand before the motor ever turns. Each step tests one thing, so a failure points at one part.

  1. Rings alone, by hand. Push the lead ring from end to end with one finger. The whole panel should follow smoothly and stack at the left. It catches at one spot = a rod joint, a burr or a centre bracket. Fix it now, or the motor will stall at the same spot.
  2. Loop on, nothing clamped. Turn the drive pulley by hand for a full lap of the cord. The cord should move around both pulleys, and the idler should sit near the middle of its slot. The idler sits at the end of the slot = the cord is too long, or it stretches; shorten it. The pulley turns under the cord = not enough tension or grip (see the slip callout).
  3. Clearance, still unclamped. Run the loop another lap while watching the hooks, the pleats at the stack end and both bracket arms. Nothing should touch the cord. A hook or bracket rubs = move the cord toward the wall or lower the pulleys. Do not move the cord toward the rings.
  4. Clamp the lead ring, drive by hand. Turn the drive pulley clockwise, seen from the front. The curtain should close. It opens instead = the lock is on the bottom run. That is fine if you note it, because the controller's directions then swap.
  5. Limit switches, by hand. Still turning the pulley by hand, go slowly to each end and listen. Each switch should click just before the lead ring meets the bracket or squeezes the stack. No click = move the switch or the bead toward the direction of travel. It clicks with a gap still showing = the curtain will stop short of fully closed; move it the other way.
  6. Only then the motor, in short bursts. One short run each way from the controller. Check the direction from step 4, and that each switch stops the motor. Then do one full open and close, and time it against the 14–24 s estimate. Much slower, or it stops midway = slip or drag. Go back to step 1 or 2 rather than fitting a bigger motor.
